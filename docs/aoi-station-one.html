§1 · Design driver

One requirement sets the whole architecture

Three of your four defect classes — missing parts, wrong position, polarity — are solved by ordinary 2D template comparison. Any camera and any even light will find them. They are not what shapes this machine.

The fourth one is. Fillet quality, tombstoning and skew are height problems being solved with a flat picture. A camera cannot measure solder volume, so the machine has to recover surface slope instead — and the only way to do that without a 3D sensor is to control the angle the light arrives from.

Molten-then-frozen solder is specular: it behaves like a curved mirror, not a diffuse surface. A mirrored surface tilted at angle α from horizontal reflects light into a vertically-mounted camera only when that light arrives at elevation θ = 90° − 2α. Turn that around and it becomes a measuring instrument:

Illumination elevation → surface slope it reveals
ChannelElevation θSlope α seenWhat that is on a joint

DIFFUSEdomeallFlat, shadowless — for body markings and OCR

Consequence

Because slope is inferred from which light was on, illumination geometry and uniformity are now metrology, not lighting. Every downstream number in this document — sensor, aperture, cycle time — is downstream of that decision.

§2 · Optics

Sensor, sampling, and why the board is scanned in tiles

Detecting a 0402 needs roughly 20 px across its 0.5 mm width; grading its fillet needs closer to 40. Setting the sampling pitch at 12 µm/px gives a 0402 body 83 × 42 px and each termination about 20 px of fillet to work with — and, usefully, it also puts a 0201 at 50 × 25 px, so the optics do not need replacing if your product mix shrinks.

A single-shot camera covering all of 150 × 150 mm at that pitch would need 156 MP. That camera costs more than the rest of the machine. The alternative is to keep a modest sensor and move it: a 4096 × 3000 sensor at 12 µm/px sees 49 × 36 mm per exposure, so a full board is covered by 20 tiles in a 4 × 5 raster — about 5.8 seconds of scanning. On an offline station with a human loading boards, that time is free.

Focal length: buying working distance to buy away parallax

A non-telecentric lens sees tall components from an angle, so a 1 mm-tall part near the tile corner appears displaced from where it really sits. That error lands directly in your placement-offset measurement, so it matters. Lengthening the lens pushes the camera further back and flattens the cone:

The residual 7.6 px is not left uncorrected: package height is already known from the component library, so the pipeline applies a per-component parallax shift from the tile centre before measuring offset. That software correction is what lets you skip a telecentric lens and its ~$1,500 premium.

Aperture: the point where sharpness stops improving

Stopping down buys depth of field until diffraction erases the detail you stopped down to get. At 12 µm sampling the two curves cross cleanly:

f/5.6 gives ±0.60 mm of focus latitude with the optical blur still smaller than one pixel. Focus the plane roughly 0.5 mm above the board and everything from the bare pad up to a 1.1 mm-tall part stays sharp. Taller parts — electrolytics, connectors, crystals — go soft in the body, but their solder joints sit on the board plane and stay sharp, so joint grading is unaffected. Only body-marking OCR on tall parts degrades, and that is a small enough set to handle with a second pass at a different focus if it ever matters.

Design constraint this creates

±0.60 mm is less than the warp a free 150 mm board can have. Vacuum hold-down is not an accessory here, it is what makes the optics work. Budget for a machined platen with a proper vacuum grid rather than a printed part with a few holes.

§3 · Illumination

Monochrome sensor, four sequential channels

Commercial AOI conventionally fires red, green and blue rings simultaneously into a colour camera, letting the Bayer filter separate the angles in one shot. It is fast, and it is the wrong trade for an offline station where you have seconds to spare.

Use a monochrome sensor and switch one ring at a time, capturing four frames per tile. It costs about 32 ms extra per tile and buys four things:

  • True full resolution per channel. A Bayer sensor interpolates — the red channel is really one pixel in four, upsampled. Sequential mono gives every channel all 12 MP, genuinely resolved.
  • Roughly 3× the sensitivity, since no colour filter array is throwing away light. Shorter exposures, less motion sensitivity.
  • No cross-talk. A red LED's tail leaks into the green Bayer pixel; separate exposures cannot leak into each other at all. This matters because the fillet grading is a ratio between channels — cross-talk is a direct measurement error.
  • Channels are no longer limited to three. The fourth is a flat diffuse dome frame for body markings and OCR, which the RGB scheme has no room for. A fifth coaxial channel can be added later for specular defects without redesigning anything.

The LEDs can then all be the same white or the same wavelength, chosen for sensor QE rather than for being separable — which also removes chromatic focus shift between channels, a nuisance the RGB approach has to live with.

Strobe, not continuous

Drive each ring as a pulse of under a millisecond, overdriven several times past its continuous rating. Peak illumination goes up sharply while average power and LED junction temperature stay low, which matters because LED output drifts with temperature and drift shows up as a slow change in your fillet thresholds. A machine that passes boards in the morning and fails them in the afternoon is a lighting thermal problem nine times out of ten.

Trigger the strobe from the camera's hardware strobe output, not from software. A microcontroller listens to the camera and switches the appropriate MOSFET; software only selects which channel is armed next. Software-timed lighting will eventually mis-pair a frame with the wrong channel under load, and that failure is maddening to diagnose.

Buy this one, don't build it

A DIY dome is tempting and the parts cost under $250. Skip it for the first unit. Illumination non-uniformity is the single largest source of false calls, and a hand-built dome makes uniformity a variable you will be debugging for months, entangled with every algorithm you write. Buy a commercial multi-angle ring or dome with a documented uniformity spec, get the machine working, then build your own once you have a reference to measure against.

§4 · Mechanics

Where precision is worth paying for, and where it isn't

The instinct on a vision machine is to buy the most repeatable stage affordable. Resist it. Every tile is aligned in software against board features before anything is measured, so stage positioning error is corrected, not accumulated. What the stage actually has to deliver is far more modest:

Mechanical requirement budget
Axis of concernRequirementWhy that number
XY repeatability±100 µmAbsorbed entirely by the 10% tile overlap plus per-component search window. A belt-driven stage clears this.
XY settle time<200 ms to <12 µmResidual vibration must fall below one pixel before exposure. This, not travel speed, sets cycle time.
Z focus stability±150 µmMust stay well inside the ±600 µm depth of field across all travel. Drives frame stiffness.
Optical axis squareness±0.2°Tilt shifts the effective illumination elevation and corrupts slope inference. This one is genuinely tight.
Board flatness on platen<200 µmVacuum-flattened. The hardest real constraint in the machine.

Note where the tight tolerances landed: not on the stage, but on frame stiffness and optical squareness. Spend money on a rigid gantry and a properly machined platen; buy an ordinary linear-rail stage. This inverts the usual budget and saves roughly $1,500.

Two other things are not optional. The enclosure must be genuinely light-tight with a matte black interior — ambient light varies with the time of day and with whether someone opened a roller shutter, and it will walk your thresholds. And the fixture area must be ESD-safe: dissipative platen surface, bonded frame, wrist-strap point at the load position. An inspection station that damages boards is worse than no station.

§5 · Software

The pipeline, and the loop that makes it improve

Inspection programs are not written per board — they are generated from data the SMT line already produces. The pick-and-place centroid file gives every reference designator its X, Y, rotation and side; the BOM maps designator to part number and package; a package library maps package to body dimensions, termination geometry and polarity-marking type. From those three, the ROI list writes itself.

Teaching is then a matter of scanning a handful of known-good boards and recording, per component, what "correct" looks like across all four channels. Runtime compares against that. The part that separates a machine people trust from one they ignore is the fourth stage:

Stack

Python with OpenCV and NumPy for the pipeline; the camera driven through GenICam so you are not married to one vendor's SDK. Motion and lighting speak to a single microcontroller over serial with a deliberately dumb protocol — move, settle, arm channel, strobe — so the timing-critical part is small enough to reason about. Models in PyTorch, small enough that inference runs on CPU in about a millisecond per crop; the GPU is for retraining overnight, not for running the line. What those models should and should not be asked to do is §6.

Build the operator interface as a native desktop application, not a web app. This machine sits on a factory floor and must work when the network does not, with input latency that does not make an operator wait. Push yield data to a server for reporting; keep inspection entirely local, with SQLite as the local record.

§6 · Learned inspection

Where machine learning belongs, and where it quietly fails

The useful question is not whether to use AI but where. There is a version of this that fails on a factory floor and a version that works, and the difference between them is a single architectural choice: does the model have to find the components, or is it handed them?

Handing them over is possible because this problem has a property most vision problems lack — the correct answer is already written down. The centroid file states that a 10 kΩ 0402 sits at (34.2, 51.7) rotated 90°. A detector that must rediscover that from pixels is solving a far harder problem than one that starts from it, and discards the strongest prior available.

Match the learning problem to data you can actually get

Defects are rare, and that single fact eliminates most of the obvious approaches. At a healthy 200 DPMO across 500 components, a board carries about one defect every ten boards. Collecting a thousand examples of “insufficient solder on 0402” means inspecting ten thousand boards — and the rarer classes never arrive at all. So the design question becomes: which learning problems can be fed from data that already exists?

Data required vs. data obtainable, per approach
ApproachTraining data neededWhere it comes fromAvailable
Anomaly detectionPatchCore / PaDiM / EfficientAD 50–200 good crops per package family. No defect examples at all. The golden boards you already scan during teach. day one
Polarity classifiersmall CNN, per package family 500–2000 crops, balanced across correct and reversed. Golden crops, with the reversed class synthesised by rotating them 180°. Perfect labels, zero annotation. day one
False-call filterbinary verifier on failed components 500–2000 adjudicated fails. Operator review clicks. Every adjudication is a label, and it only ever sees components the rule layer already failed — so the classes stay roughly balanced. 4–8 weeks
Supervised defect detectorthe intuitive approach 1000+ per defect class × 8 classes × ~15 package families. Nowhere. You would need six figures of boards, and the rare classes still would not fill. never

A · Anomaly detection is the highest-value model in the machine

Because it inverts the data problem. Instead of learning what defects look like, it learns what normal looks like and flags departures — a memory bank of feature embeddings from good patches, scored by distance to the nearest normal neighbour. It is trained entirely from the golden boards you already have to scan, so it costs no additional data collection whatsoever, and it catches failure modes nobody thought to write a rule for. This class of method is what industrial inspection benchmarks are built around, and it is the right first model to implement.

B · Polarity is where supervised learning cleanly beats rules — and the labels are free

Rules struggle with polarity because the cue differs per part: a cathode stripe here, a moulded pin-1 dimple there, a printed dot somewhere else. A small network on the aligned patch handles all of them uniformly. The trick that makes it practical is that the negative class can be manufactured exactly — rotate a known-good crop 180° and you have a perfectly labelled reversed part. The same applies to gross misplacement (translate the crop) and missing components (composite the bare-pad background). Three defect classes with unlimited free training data.

C · The false-call filter is what actually moves the number in §8

Run the rule and anomaly layers first, then train a second model on operator-adjudicated crops that answers only one question: was this call real? It sees a naturally abundant and roughly balanced dataset, because it only ever looks at components that already failed. This is the standard route from a mediocre false-call rate to a production one.

It is also the one component that can create escapes, so it gets gated rather than trusted: allowed to convert FAIL to PASS only above a high confidence threshold, with a weekly audit of a random sample of everything it suppressed. A model permitted to silently overrule the inspection is a model that will eventually overrule a real defect.

Fuse with a tree, not a vote

The obvious move is to let rules and model vote and take a majority. Do not. Feed the rule measurements — low-band pixel fraction, NCC score, offset in microns, gap specular response — as features alongside the anomaly score into a small gradient-boosted tree. It trains on hundreds of samples rather than thousands, produces calibrated confidence rather than a raw score, and reports feature importances, so when a package type starts misbehaving you can see which measurement drifted. That last property is what makes the system debuggable eighteen months from now.

Foundation models: useful, just not for the verdict

A vision-language model looking at a solder fillet at 12 µm/px will not beat a purpose-trained six-layer network, will be orders of magnitude slower, and will occasionally state something confident and wrong. Keep them out of the inspection decision. They earn their place on the work around it, which is genuinely tedious:

  • Normalising line data. Centroid and BOM exports differ between every EDA tool and every contract manufacturer. Parsing them into one schema is exactly the kind of messy, format-tolerant work language models do well and rule-based parsers do badly.
  • Building the package library from datasheets. Extracting body dimensions, termination geometry and polarity-marking convention out of PDF datasheets, which otherwise is weeks of manual data entry.
  • Explaining a failure to an operator in a sentence, from the underlying measurements, rather than showing a bare score.
  • Root cause across time. Summarising defect trends against process events — noticing that tombstones on one capacitor cluster rose after a paste lot changed.

What this changes in the build plan

The learned layer stops being a final phase. Anomaly detection and the polarity classifier both train off golden-board data, so they land in Phase 3 alongside teach, not in Phase 6 — and the machine starts more capable than a purely rule-based one would. Only the false-call filter has to wait for production labels. No change to the bill of materials: 500 components × four channels of 96×96 patches is well under a second per board on the specified GPU, and the small models run on CPU in about a millisecond per crop.

§7 · Edge deployment

Raspberry Pi, and the right job for YOLO

These are two separate questions with genuinely different answers. The Pi is viable — the arithmetic works out better than you might expect. YOLO as a whole-board defect detector is not, and one number settles it.

The number that settles the YOLO question

A detector runs at a fixed input size, typically 640×640. Your tile is 4096×3000. Feeding one to the other means a 6.4× downscale, which takes the sampling pitch from 12 µm/px to 77 µm/px. At that pitch a 0402 solder fillet is under four pixels wide. There is no algorithm — none — that grades a fillet from four pixels. The information was destroyed before the model ran.

So use YOLO — on the crop, not the board

None of this argues against the Ultralytics toolchain, which is genuinely good: easy training, clean export, first-class accelerator support. Keep it. Just point it at a different input. Run the detection head on the whole board where objects are large and few, and the classification and segmentation heads on the CAD-cropped patch where the resolution still exists.

The segmentation row is the most interesting one. Using YOLO-seg to isolate the solder region on each pad, then computing the §1 slope-band fractions inside that mask rather than inside a fixed rectangle, removes the pipeline's biggest source of noise — pad regions that clip a neighbouring component or catch a bit of silkscreen. That is YOLO doing something it is genuinely better at than a threshold.

The Pi holds up — with four constraints

The architecture in §5 turns out to be unusually Pi-friendly, because all the heavy work already happens inside small CAD-defined patches rather than across full frames. Streaming tile by tile, peak memory is about 49 MB — nothing. Four things do need designing around:

  • Use a USB3 camera, not GigE. A board is roughly 1 GB of raw frames. Over the Pi's single Gigabit port that is about 9 seconds of pure transfer; over USB3 it is about 2.6. This one choice matters more than anything else in the Pi build.
  • Store crops, not frames. Keeping full frames is a gigabyte per board and will fill any disk within a week. Keep the 96×96 patches instead — about 5 MB compressed per board — and retain full frames only for components that failed. That is also exactly the data the retraining loop needs.
  • Mind the single PCIe lane. The Pi 5 has one PCIe x1, and both an AI accelerator and an NVMe drive want it. Simplest resolution: accelerator on PCIe, storage on an external USB3 SSD. Avoid PCIe switch HATs on a machine that has to be reliable.
  • The Pi cannot train. It runs inference only. Retraining the classifiers and refitting the fusion model happens on a separate machine — any ordinary desktop with a GPU — and compiled models get pushed to the station. This is a normal edge split, but it means the workstation does not fully disappear from the budget, it just moves off the machine and gets shared across stations.

One deployment note specific to accelerators: models must be quantised to int8 to run on a Hailo-class device, and quantisation error is not free on a task this close to measurement. Validate post-quantisation accuracy against the float model on a held-out set before trusting it — a two-point drop in classifier accuracy is a meaningful change in false call rate. Also prefer a purely convolutional anomaly model such as EfficientAD over a memory-bank method like PatchCore here, since the former compiles cleanly to the accelerator while the latter leaves a nearest-neighbour search stranded on the CPU.

What it costs

Swapping the workstation for a Pi edge node takes $1,368 out of the station, and adds one training machine shared across every station you build. Both configurations are costed line by line in §9 — that table is the single source of truth for price, not this section.

Recommendation

Develop on the workstation, deploy on the Pi. During Phases 1–5 you will re-run the pipeline over the same boards hundreds of times while tuning, and a Pi makes every one of those iterations slower — which shows up as weeks, not minutes. Because the architecture is CAD-anchored and patch-based, the port is a deployment step rather than a rewrite: the same models, quantised and recompiled. Freeze the pipeline first, then move it.

If the goal is several stations, the Pi is clearly right and the case gets stronger with each unit. If it is one station, build it on the workstation and treat the Pi as a Phase 7 cost-reduction.

§8 · Acceptance

False call rate is the only number that decides this

AOI projects rarely fail because they miss defects. They fail because they cry wolf, operators learn to click through, and within a month the machine is a very expensive conveyor. On a 500-component board, the per-component false-call probability compounds fast:

False calls per 500-component board, and what happens
Per-component FCRFalse calls / boardOperator reality
0.05%0.25Commercial-grade. One review every four boards.
0.2%1.0Target for this build. Roughly 20 s of review per board.
1%5.0Tolerable but disliked. Review becomes the bottleneck.
5%25Machine gets ignored. Operators rubber-stamp; escapes rise above the no-AOI baseline.

Expect the rule-based pipeline alone to land somewhere around 3–8% on real production boards. That is not a failure of the design — it is the normal starting point, and it is exactly why the operator feedback loop exists. Getting from there to under 1% is a data problem solved over weeks of production, not an algorithm problem solved in a sprint. Plan the schedule around that tuning period rather than treating first-light as completion.

Track three numbers from day one, per package type: false call rate, escape rate against a periodically hand-inspected sample, and review time per board. Package-level breakdown is what tells you where to spend effort — it is usually two or three troublesome packages generating most of the noise.

§10 · Build plan

Phases, with exit criteria that mean something

Each phase below ends on a test you can objectively fail. Resist starting the next phase until the current one passes — the failure mode on machines like this is a half-calibrated rig that everyone keeps writing algorithms against.

  1. Mechanical build and optical alignment

    3–4 weeks

    Frame, gantry, vacuum platen, enclosure. Camera and lens mounted, squared, focused. No software beyond a live view.

    Exit — A chrome-on-glass dot grid resolves at 12 ±0.3 µm/px across the full tile, corner to corner, with distortion mapped and under 0.5 px after correction. Optical axis square to the platen within 0.2°.

  2. Illumination and capture chain

    3–4 weeks

    Dome mounted, four channels wired, hardware strobe synchronised to the camera. Automated tile raster with settle detection.

    Exit — Channel uniformity within ±5% across the tile. The same board scanned 20 times over an 8-hour period varies less than 2% in per-channel mean intensity. Frame-to-channel pairing never slips under sustained load.

  3. Program generation and teach

    2–3 weeks

    Centroid and BOM import, package library, automatic ROI generation, fiducial alignment, golden-board capture and statistics. Because they train on golden data alone, the anomaly model and the polarity classifier of §6 are built here too.

    Exit — A real production assembly imports and generates correct ROIs for over 98% of designators with no manual editing, verified by overlay against a scanned board.

  4. Rule-based inspection

    4–6 weeks

    All eight tests implemented. Parallax correction. Verdict fusion and per-component confidence.

    Exit — On a seeded board with 30 deliberately induced defects across all four classes, catches at least 28. False call rate may still be high; do not tune it yet.

  5. Operator station and traceability

    3–4 weeks

    Review screen, defect map, serial scanning, SQLite records, yield reporting. Every adjudicated verdict stored as a labelled crop.

    Exit — An operator with 30 minutes of training reviews a full board's fails in under 60 seconds and the labels land correctly in the dataset.

  6. False-call filter and tuning to production

    8–14 weeks

    The false-call filter trained on accumulated operator labels; fusion tree fitted per package; retraining pipeline automated. This runs alongside real production and is the only stage that cannot start earlier.

    Exit — Under 1% per-component false call rate sustained across your three highest-volume assemblies, with escape rate measured against hand inspection and no worse than current manual QC.

Total: roughly 6–9 months with one dedicated engineer, or 4–5 months with a mechanical/electrical person and a vision-software person working in parallel. Phase 6 is the one that gets underestimated; it is also the one that determines whether the machine is used.

§11 · Limits

What this machine will not do

Worth being explicit about, so nobody discovers it at month five:

  • No true height measurement. Slope inference grades fillets well and catches gross coplanarity problems, but it does not measure solder volume. If you later need real volumetric grading or coplanarity to a spec, that is a 3D sensor — laser triangulation or fringe projection — and a different machine.
  • Nothing under a BGA. No optical method sees hidden joints. BGA voiding and opens need X-ray, full stop.
  • Bottom side needs a second pass. Single camera, single side. Double-sided assemblies get flipped and scanned again.
  • Fine-pitch IC leads are marginal. A 0.5 mm-pitch QFP lead lands at about 21 px, workable for presence and gross bridging but not for lead coplanarity or subtle wetting.
  • Tall components go soft. Bodies above roughly 1.1 mm fall outside the depth of field; their joints stay sharp, but marking OCR on tall parts will need a second focus pass if you want it.

One last piece of arithmetic worth having in the room. This build saves perhaps $20–35k in capital against an entry-level commercial AOI, and costs 6–9 engineer-months. Purely on capex-versus-labour that is close to a wash. It becomes clearly worth doing if you will build more than one unit, if you need inspection behaviour a commercial machine's closed software will not give you, or if in-house vision capability is itself something you want to own. You have already made that call — this document assumes it and does not relitigate it. But it is worth confirming that at least one of those three reasons genuinely applies before Phase 1 starts, because the answer changes nothing about the design and everything about whether to build it.
